# Building a Healthcare Cybersecurity Program from the Ground Up

**Security leadership case study | Healthcare | Program development and risk reduction**

> **Portfolio note:** This is a retrospective, sanitized account of professional experience. It does not disclose employer-specific security architecture, configurations, incident details, or internal reports. Results are self-reported career accomplishments, not independently audited portfolio measurements.

## Executive summary

I was responsible for establishing an information security function at a regional healthcare organization rather than taking over a mature department. The work required building a team, assessing existing capabilities, introducing practical security processes, and demonstrating measurable progress to leadership while respecting the operational demands of healthcare.

I began by hiring **two security analysts** and conducting a gap assessment of the existing toolset, particularly its ability to support **alerting, troubleshooting, and triage**. Vulnerability management emerged as a major gap. I led the introduction of a scanning platform, regular scanning, cross-functional remediation processes, and executive reporting.

**Reported outcomes:**

- **82% reduction in open critical-, high-, and medium-severity vulnerabilities in 12 months**, prioritizing exploitable critical findings first.
- Security team growth from **two initial analysts to a 10-person team** over the course of building the function.
- Phishing susceptibility reduced from **22% to 0.2%** through security awareness initiatives (a separate program outcome; measurement period not specified here).
- **Quarterly leadership presentations** using dashboards covering vulnerability counts, severity, remediation progress, and trends.

## The challenge

Starting a security function from scratch meant addressing staffing, policies, processes, technology gaps, and leadership support at the same time. In a healthcare setting, improvements also had to account for system availability and the needs of clinical operations.

The question was not simply which tools to purchase. It was how to establish a sustainable security operation that could identify risks, help the organization act on them, and explain progress in business terms.

## Step 1: Hire and assess

I hired two analysts to establish the initial team, then assessed the existing security toolset for its ability to support alerting, troubleshooting, and triage. That assessment surfaced vulnerability management as a significant gap and helped focus the next investment and process decisions.

## Step 2: Build risk-based vulnerability management

I led the implementation of a vulnerability scanning platform and regular scanning activities. We coordinated remediation with infrastructure and other IT teams and tracked open findings and progress.

Remediation was **risk-prioritized**: exploitable critical vulnerabilities came first, followed by other critical findings and then high- and medium-severity findings. The objective was not to generate more findings; it was to reduce meaningful exposure through repeatable work and shared accountability.

## Step 3: Measure and communicate

We developed dashboards and recurring reporting showing vulnerability counts, severity, remediation progress, and trends. I presented progress to leadership **quarterly**, connecting technical findings with remediation priorities and organizational risk.

Over **12 months**, the number of **open critical-, high-, and medium-severity vulnerabilities fell by 82%**. This figure describes the tracked open findings in those severity categories, not all possible vulnerabilities or a claim of eliminating organizational risk.

## Step 4: Grow the broader security function

The team ultimately grew to **10 people**. Alongside the vulnerability management work, the broader program developed security policies, operating practices, and security awareness initiatives. One separately tracked awareness outcome was a reduction in phishing susceptibility from **22% to 0.2%**.

## What I learned

- **Start with people and visibility.** An initial team and a clear understanding of existing capabilities make later technology decisions more effective.
- **Prioritize by risk.** Exploitability and severity should inform the remediation queue, especially where changes must be coordinated with operational teams.
- **Make remediation a shared responsibility.** Security identifies and explains risks, but sustainable progress depends on system owners and technical partners.
- **Measure outcomes, not tool deployments.** Leadership should be able to see whether exposure is declining and where work remains.
- **Respect the business context.** Healthcare security controls must be effective without unnecessarily disrupting clinical operations.

## How this translates to a new security operations role

In a new environment, I would begin with an assessment of people, visibility, detection and response processes, and priority risks. I would then establish a practical roadmap, identify measurable outcomes, and report progress in terms leaders can act on. The exact sequencing would depend on the organization's environment and constraints.

## Scope and limitations

This is a leadership case study, not a technical implementation guide. The precise scanning product, internal asset inventory, remediation SLAs, vulnerability counts, and operational architecture are intentionally omitted. The 82% outcome is a 12-month reduction in **open** findings across the named severity categories; it is not a production benchmark for other organizations. The phishing metric describes a separate awareness initiative and should not be confused with vulnerability reduction.
